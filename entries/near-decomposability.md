---
id: near-decomposability
claim: Complex systems tend to be built as hierarchies of nearly independent subsystems, which is why studying their parts in isolation works as far as it does, and why it eventually stops working.
evidence:
  level: extension
  kinds: [theory, observation]
  why: Simon's argument rests on examples across physics, biology and organizations and on a formal argument about evolution, not on a systematic survey.
agreement:
  status: leaning
  why: The idea is foundational in systems science, organization theory and software design and is seldom disputed as a tendency. How general it is remains open.
  wouldChange: Evidence that many important complex systems are not nearly decomposable (for example, densely interconnected networks where no stable subsystems exist).
objection: Many systems of current concern, such as global financial networks and ecosystems under stress, show strong cross-level coupling, so treating them as decomposable can hide exactly the interactions that cause failure.
unknowns: How to tell in advance when near-decomposability holds for a particular system and when it breaks down.
