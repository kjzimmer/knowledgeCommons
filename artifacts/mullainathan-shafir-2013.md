## Summary

Argues that scarcity of any kind (money, time, food, companionship) captures the mind: it narrows attention to the
pressing need ("tunnelling") and taxes "bandwidth", the mental capacity left for everything else, which can lead to
choices that deepen scarcity. Draws on the authors' experiments, including studies with farmers before and after
harvest. Some of the underlying findings have faced replication questions (not yet checked in the commons).
